Qualitative Research

AI Transparency & User Trust

Participants read clarity and structure as cues that ChatGPT’s answers were accurate. Their sense of what it could do, and how it came across, took shape during the conversation itself.

Role
Solo researcher
Participants
4 adults, ages 25–58
Methods
Contextual inquiry, think-aloud, semi-structured interviews, thematic analysis
Course
Emerging Practices in HCI, Fall 2025

Note on scope: This was a small, exploratory study (n=4), with participants recruited through convenience sampling. Hence, the following findings are not intended to be generalizable. Tasks were also brief and covered low-stakes topics chosen by participants. Whether these patterns hold for higher-stakes tasks would require further investigation. The study’s value is in the conceptual direction it reveals, particularly in how transparency is perceived and built through interaction, and how trust is signaled.

The question

How do users perceive transparency in ChatGPT’s explanations, and how does that perception shape their trust?

Transparency: how clearly a system communicates how it reached an answer and where its limits are.

ChatGPT and similar large language models (LLMs) are non-deterministic: the same input can produce varying outputs across interactions. As such, no single output can stand in for how the system behaves. Understanding how people come to trust it means looking at patterns across their interactions with it, and for a researcher, that means observing how people read and react to the responses they actually receive, which is what this study set out to do.

Unlike a search engine, which returns links users can follow and check, LLMs produce synthesized answers that often come without visible citations or traceable reasoning.

This study was conducted in fall 2025, utilizing the version of ChatGPT that was available at the time as the case example. It looked at how perceived transparency shapes trust during brief, task-based interactions. During that period, ChatGPT only showed sources when it searched the web, an action it did not perform in any of the study sessions.

Method

Each participant completed a short contextual inquiry followed immediately by a semi-structured interview. The sessions were brief by design, and participants chose their own topics.

Participants were four adults, ages 25 to 58, recruited through convenience sampling. Their experience with AI varied, from regular ChatGPT use, to using it for the first time, to familiarity with other tools such as Gemini through Google’s AI Overviews.

  • Contextual inquiry (~5 min)
  • Think-aloud protocol
  • Semi-structured interview (~5 min)
  • Inductive thematic analysis
  • Convenience sampling

During the contextual inquiry, participants prompted ChatGPT to explain a topic of their own choosing, something they were curious about or didn’t fully understand, and were encouraged to think aloud while interacting naturally. The goal was to surface their mental models and assumptions about the system, and to reveal which cues they used to assess the credibility of the output.

Sessions were recorded with informed consent, transcribed, and supplemented with observation notes, which together formed the data for thematic analysis.

Because each participant chose their own topic, the responses varied in structure and tone, which made it possible to see how participants read those differences.

What I found

Thematic analysis of the contextual inquiry and interview sessions revealed two major themes.

Theme 01

Users infer accuracy from presentation cues

Participants frequently used stylistic and structural features, such as clarity, logical sequencing, level of detail, and fitting technical language, as cues for accuracy. None of the responses in these sessions showed citations, and no participant brought them up.

This held even when participants acknowledged they couldn’t verify the content themselves. Matching the register of the question carried weight as well.

The participant who asked how to build a paper airplane valued the logical, step-by-step sequence in the same way.

Theme 02

Mental models of transparency develop through interaction

Whatever their prior experience with AI, participants’ sense of what ChatGPT could and couldn’t do kept developing through interaction with the system itself. Some of it formed while reading the first response, before any follow-up.

Responses that gave more than participants expected also showed them more of what the system could do, which shaped the questions they asked or planned to ask next. The participant who asked how to play D&D noted that ChatGPT’s overview simplified the game helpfully, and that more detail would take further prompting.

The participant who asked how to build a paper airplane said they would try ChatGPT’s instructions first and come back to it if the results weren’t satisfactory. Like the D&D participant, they treated the conversation as something to return to as their needs changed.

Length and tone came up throughout. The participant who asked about the moon had expected a more direct answer than the one they got.

The D&D participant found the “chaos gremlins” framing in ChatGPT’s explanation distracting and tonally mismatched for an informational context. Flair like this got in the way of the explanation, lowering its perceived transparency without changing how confident the participant felt about its accuracy.

Design implications

The findings suggest that participants’ perceptions of transparency were shaped less by explanations of how the system reasoned and more by how information was presented. Here are three directions for design.

Prioritize structure and clarity

Because participants inferred accuracy from presentation cues, explanations should prioritize clear organization, logical sequencing, and a level of detail that fits the question. Participants valued step-by-step formats with clear hierarchy where the task called for it.

Support conversational probing

Participants learned what the system could do from its responses, starting with the first. The dragon participant wasn’t sure what to expect when they asked how to draw a dragon. The depth, detail, and step-by-step clarity of the response pleasantly surprised them, showing that the system could handle that kind of question and that they didn’t need to narrow what they asked. Suggested follow-up prompts, or an option to expand on detail, could make that range visible earlier in the interaction.

Align tone with the task

Conversational flair may suit some requests, but one participant found the humor in their D&D explanation distracting in an informational context, especially since it was unexpected. Tone should adapt to what the user is asking for.

Limitations and ethical considerations

Risk of overreliance

Participants read clarity, structure, tone, and level of detail as cues of accuracy, and lack of citations did not appear to lower their trust. Their tasks were low-stakes, and they felt little pressure to double-check. No response in these sessions was clearly wrong, so this risk is projected from the pattern. When presentation alone signals reliability, users may accept incorrect information without checking it. The same habits could carry over to more consequential questions, for example in health or law, where a confident, well-organized wrong answer can cause real harm.

A response that signals uncertainty, or is transparent about the extent of its knowledge, gives people a reason to check before trusting it, which makes balancing clarity with communicating limits an important part of designing these systems.

These findings come from the kind of question participants brought: general knowledge, easily verified elsewhere, and not complex or controversial. For topics where the answer depends on which source you consult, disclosure may accomplish what structure cannot. Testing that, along with whether these patterns hold on a larger scale and in tasks where being wrong has more significance, would take a follow-up study.

Reflection

What surprised me most was how readily participants trusted the responses, especially without citations. I hadn’t expected citations to go entirely unmentioned, though it makes sense for low-stakes questions with fairly common answers. Tone and structure mattered more than I anticipated, and the presentation of information did a lot of the heavy lifting.

Participants’ understanding of what the system could do also took shape within the responses themselves. When a response was more thorough than they expected, it showed them more of the system’s capabilities, which led them to plan further questions. Disclosure still serves a purpose. However, it’s outside this study’s scope to determine how well it does so. What the study does show is that, at the time, the responses carried much of how participants came to understand the system, which highlights the value of designing for that deliberately.

In a follow-up or larger-scale study, I would want to look at more complex or controversial tasks, where there isn’t a black-and-white answer, and at higher-stakes tasks, where misplaced trust costs more. I would also want to explore where flair is appropriate, and what kind.
