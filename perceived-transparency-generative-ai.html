AI Transparency & User Trust

Perceived Transparency in Generative AI

HCI 5960: Emerging Practices in Human-Computer Interaction · Fall 2025

A qualitative study examining how users evaluate ChatGPT's credibility during explanatory tasks, and why structure and tone carry more weight than citations.

Note on scope: This is a small, illustrative study (n=4) using brief, low-stakes tasks and convenience sampling. The findings are not intended to be generalizable. The value is in the conceptual direction they open up, particularly in how transparency in AI is experienced through interaction rather than built into individual responses.

The Question

ChatGPT and similar large language models are non-deterministic: the same input can produce meaningfully different outputs across interactions. This creates a genuine challenge for users trying to assess whether a response is accurate, and for researchers trying to understand how trust in these systems actually forms.

Unlike a search engine, which returns links users can verify, LLMs produce synthesized responses that feel authoritative but often come without visible citations or traceable reasoning. How do users decide whether to trust them? What signals are they actually reading?

This study used ChatGPT as a case example to explore those questions, specifically looking at how perceived transparency shapes user trust during brief, task-based interactions.

4 participants, ages 25–58
~10 min per session (task + interview)
2 themes from thematic analysis

Method

Each participant completed a short contextual inquiry followed immediately by a semi-structured interview. The sessions were deliberately brief and low-stakes to reflect everyday AI use rather than high-consequence scenarios.

Contextual inquiry (~5 min) Think-aloud protocol Semi-structured interview (~5 min) Inductive thematic analysis Convenience sampling

During the contextual inquiry, participants were asked to prompt ChatGPT to explain a topic of their own choosing, something they were genuinely curious about or didn't fully understand. They were encouraged to think aloud while interacting naturally. The goal was to surface the mental models and trust signals participants were actually using in the moment.

Participant tasks and trust signals
Topic chosen Response they received What drove their trust judgment
How to draw a dragon Step-by-step instructions with fine details like wing angle and shadows Detail level, including suggestions they hadn't considered, despite having no way to judge the AI's drawing knowledge
How to build a paper airplane Sequential build steps, plus alternatives for different flight goals Steps followed logically from one to the next
Why the moon appears larger at times Long structured explanation with scientific terminology and statistics Register matched the question; statistics made it sound more legitimate; the length was more than they wanted
How to play Dungeons & Dragons Short summary, then a sectioned overview with informal flair The topic's popularity and the volume of information available elsewhere; the "chaos gremlins" framing read as distracting

The variety mattered. It produced different response structures from the AI and exposed how users read those structural differences.

What I Found

Analysis surfaced two consistent themes.

Theme 01

Users infer accuracy from presentation cues, not citations

Participants consistently used stylistic and structural features (clarity, logical sequencing, level of detail, appropriate use of technical language) as proxies for accuracy. No participant raised citations as a trust signal, and the absence of citations didn't reduce confidence.

This happened even when participants acknowledged they couldn't personally verify the content. The response felt credible, so it was treated as credible.

"I actually had no idea whether they would know how to draw a dragon, but they seem to be giving me fantastic advice."

— Participant asking ChatGPT for drawing guidance

"I think it's pretty accurate. I mean, it gives me details. It gives me things I hadn't even thought of."

— Same participant, after reviewing the step-by-step response

"Yeah, I trust it. I asked a pretty scientific question, and I got a pretty scientific answer."

— Participant who asked about why the moon appears larger at certain times

"I think it just logically seemed to make sense. The steps seemed to follow one after the other and made sense."

— Participant building the paper airplane

Coherence and sequential structure were functioning as a heuristic for truth, which works adequately for low-stakes tasks, but raises real concerns for higher-stakes use cases where confident presentation could mask inaccurate content.

Theme 02

Transparency is co-constructed through interaction, not built into responses

None of the participants began with a stable, fixed mental model of how ChatGPT worked. Instead, their sense of what the system could and couldn't do, and how much to trust it, evolved through the interaction itself. Participants used follow-up questions to probe limitations, correct misalignments, and calibrate expectations.

"It was general enough, and the game itself is popular enough that I felt confident in its accuracy because there's so much available information about it."

— D&D participant, explaining why they trusted the overview despite needing follow-ups for specifics

One participant explicitly noted that the D&D response simplified things helpfully but that additional detail would require further prompting, and described already planning which follow-up questions to ask. Another said they would try the paper airplane instructions first and return to ChatGPT if the results weren't satisfactory. Both treated the conversation as an ongoing process of verification rather than a one-time lookup.

Tone was also a factor in trust calibration. One participant critiqued the "chaos gremlins" framing in ChatGPT's D&D explanation as distracting and tonally off for an instructional context, a signal that users are assessing reliability based on how things are said, not just what is said.

"I didn't expect the big info dump... I had hoped for a more straightforward answer."

— Participant who asked about the moon, noting that response structure didn't match expectations

Design Implications

The findings suggest that transparency in AI explanatory contexts is experienced through clarity and interactivity, not through explicit disclosure of model reasoning. Three directions follow from this:

Structure over disclosure

Prioritize clear organization, logical sequencing, and calibrated detail. Step-by-step formats with visible hierarchy consistently drove trust more than any other signal.

This held true for the kind of question participants brought: general knowledge, easily verified elsewhere, not complex or controversial. There was little consequence in being wrong. For other topics, where the answer may depend on which source you consult, disclosure may accomplish what structure cannot. This study didn't test that.

Support conversational probing

Users already rely on follow-up questions to refine understanding. Suggested follow-up prompts, expandable detail layers, or scope indicators would help users probe more effectively.

Align tone to context

Stylistic choices significantly influenced perceived reliability. Humor and flair that reduce overwhelm in casual contexts become credibility liabilities in instructional or explanatory tasks.

The deeper concern: When presentation quality alone drives trust, users may accept inaccurate information without flagging it, especially in low-stakes contexts where they feel no pressure to verify externally. This behavior is unlikely to be safe at scale in higher-stakes domains like health or legal contexts, where a confident, well-organized wrong answer carries real consequences. The design question isn't just how to make AI feel transparent. It's how to make it behave differently when the stakes change.

If I Were Advising the Product Team

These findings point to a reframe: transparency isn't only a disclosure problem. It's an interaction design problem. Footnotes weren't what these users were reading. They were reading structure, and treating the first response as the start of a conversation rather than the end of a lookup.

For a team building a product like ChatGPT, three starting points:

01Audit for structural clarity, not just accuracy

Users are making trust decisions based on presentation before they evaluate content. Response format is a UX problem in its own right.

02Design follow-up affordances as a trust feature

The ability to ask a follow-up question was the single most consistent trust signal across participants, and it deserves treatment as a core interaction.

03Test tone calibration by domain

Users expected different registers for scientific vs. creative tasks. A mismatch broke confidence even when the content was correct.

Reflection

Users were working to actively construct transparency for themselves rather than passively receiving it from the system. That's the part I didn't anticipate going in, and the follow-up question playing such a significant role in user trust is what reframes the design problem. Disclosure still has a job, and it's outside this study's scope to say whether that job is being done well. What it does say is that at the time of this study, the conversational loop was carrying an unexpected amount of trust weight. Making that loop more efficient and intuitive means users are better supported as they probe the system to meet their specific needs.

What this study can't tell you is whether these patterns hold at scale or in higher-stakes tasks. Those are questions for a larger follow-up, with more participants and tasks where being wrong actually costs something.
