Efficiency of Team Interactions at a Physical Therapy Clinic

On the busier of two observed days, communication between the clinic’s therapist, assistant, and reception roles dropped overall, with fewer efficient exchanges and the same number of inefficient ones. High-stress events, marked by frustration, persistent demands, or conflict, rose from 3 to 57.

Role
Event coding and the decision ladder; wrote and presented the metrics and decision ladder sections; wrote the conclusion
Team
3 researchers
Scale
219 coded events over 2 sessions (6h 41m)
Course
Cognitive Engineering, Fall 2024

Overview

At a physical therapy clinic in Egypt, this study focused on how three roles worked together: the therapist, the assistant, and the receptionist. Staff in these roles coordinated patient care as patients came and went, moving them between treatment partitions, sharing equipment, managing the schedule, and passing messages between rooms. We looked at how information moved between the roles, and how their communication changed with patient volume.

One teammate conducted the on-site observations across two evening sessions, one on a high-volume day and one on a lower-volume day, and took photos to document the environment staff worked in. Another teammate and I coded and analyzed the event logs, recording 219 events across three metrics we developed after the first session: stress, clinic vibe, and communication efficiency.

The system and its people

Several people worked in each role, and the therapist and assistant observed most closely also took the stress survey. Each role had defined responsibilities, but they overlapped, and that flexibility was essential because patient flow was unpredictable: on busy days therapists took on assistant tasks, and assistants supported both therapists and reception. Patients were present throughout but weren’t treated as part of the team, since they didn’t contribute to carrying out its work.

Therapist

Develops and delivers treatment plans and oversees patient exercises. Takes on assistant duties on busy days.

Assistant

Prepares equipment, moves patients between partitions, monitors the scheduling system, and relays information between the therapist and reception.

Receptionist

Handles scheduling, relays patient information, and coordinates patient flow, mostly by speaking in person or by phone.

Team members knew each other’s roles well, and professionalism toward patients stayed positive on both days, with 78 of 80 professional vibe events coded positive. Against that baseline, the inefficiencies pointed to how information was routed between roles.

Methods

Research on physical therapy teams was sparse, and most of it focused on individual skills and patient outcomes, so we took an exploratory approach that combined qualitative content analysis of the observations with counts of coded events and a short stress survey.

  • Content analysis
  • Direct observation
  • Timestamped event logging
  • Decision ladder
  • Occupational Stress Index (modified)

Observations covered two evening shifts: a high-volume day with more patients and more urgency (3 hours 15 minutes), and a lower-volume day with a more relaxed pace (3 hours 26 minutes). Interactions were logged with timestamps as they happened.

Two of the staff members we observed most often, a therapist and an assistant, also answered seven items adapted from the Occupational Stress Index (Srivastava & Singh, 1981), drawn from the subscales for role overload, strenuous working conditions, and responsibility for persons.

I built a decision ladder (Rasmussen, 1974) to map the patient-arrival procedure we observed and show where staff took shortcuts.

Metrics

We worked bottom up. Through observation, we found patterns in how staff communicated with each other, patients, and the clinic environment. After the first day, we chose three metrics based on those patterns, what we could assess in the data, and their potential impact on team efficiency, then refined each metric’s codes over repeated rounds of coding. We kept a code only when every coder agreed on it.

Stress

Counted as high- or low-stress events, marked when staff showed stress through what they said, their body language, or the surrounding context. High stress was marked by expressed frustration, persistent demands, or conflict, and low stress by staff relaxing, resting, or joking.

Clinic vibe

Events coded as positive or negative for the clinic’s atmosphere, and as space (physical elements such as cleanliness, sound, and privacy) or professional (the therapist’s attentiveness and friendliness toward patients). Each one either involved a patient or was something a patient could reasonably notice, as it happened or afterward, such as whether a space had been cleaned.

Communication efficiency

Exchanges between staff, coded as efficient (relevant, clear, logical, and helping toward a goal) or inefficient (unclear, inaccurate, or not helping toward one), and as informational or logistical.

Key findings

High-stress events rose from 3 to 57 on the high-volume day

Of the 57 high-stress events on the high-volume day, 35 came from two routine event types: patient check-ins, meaning any time the therapist or assistant checked on one of their patients (27), and buzzer calls from patients asking for attention (8). Both were coded as stressful only on the high-volume day, because only then did staff respond to them with visible frustration or discomfort. With more patients in care at once, check-ins came more often, 27 against 6. The buzzer rang 8 times on each day, yet only drew that response when the clinic was busy. This suggests patient volume may moderate how stressful otherwise routine events become, though the study couldn’t test it.

Efficient exchanges dropped on the busier day, while inefficient ones stayed the same

There was less communication overall on the high-volume day, 34 events against 51 on the lower-volume day. Inefficient exchanges stayed at 15 on each day, while efficient ones fell from 36 to 19, so the percentage of interactions that were inefficient rose from 29.4% to 44.1%.

“Games of telephone” made up nearly half of the busy day’s inefficient exchanges

A game of telephone was any chain where a message passed through all three team members when a direct route was available. Seven of the 15 inefficient exchanges on the high-volume day were telephone chains, against 2 of 15 on the lower-volume day. Each one was minor on its own, but alongside check-ins that kept pulling staff between patients, they added to the interruptions on the day the team was busiest.

OSI scores were consistent with moderate stress

Against the modified norms, both the therapist and the assistant scored as moderately stressed. Both rated responsibility for persons highest, at 4 of 5. The therapist’s scores ran higher overall, averaging 3.29 to the assistant’s 2.29, with role overload at 3.67. With two respondents and a shortened scale, the scores lend some support to the observations without confirming them.

Decision ladder: where shortcuts appeared

The decision ladder maps the steps staff take when a patient arrives: being notified of the arrival, reviewing the treatment plan, assessing the patient’s current status, deciding whether to adjust the plan, preparing the space and equipment, and guiding the patient through therapy. Two shortcuts emerged, likely as a result of stress or inefficiency.

Skipping equipment planning. Failing to plan the use of equipment caused delays when the machine a patient needed was already in use.

Skipping file review. Staff frequently skipped reviewing the patient’s file and moved straight into the treatment plan, which could result in them missing something relevant to the patient’s care. We were unaware of any mistakes resulting from it, which suggests the shortcut may instead reflect the staff’s expertise.

Design implications

Here are two directions for design.

Restructure communication routing

The telephone chains suggest messages often ran through the assistant, even when a direct route to reception was available. Direct routing for patient-flow messages, or a shared view of who is waiting and which partition is free, may cut relay steps. Any change would need to start with the scheduling software the team coordinated through, which deserves its own assessment.

Design for the busy days

The team worked smoothly at lower volume. The trouble came at high volume, when routine events turned stressful and efficient exchanges declined. Status displays every role can see, notifications that don’t need a verbal check-in, or clearer cues for partition assignments might reduce interruptions when the clinic is busiest.

Limitations

These findings describe one team, in one clinic, on two evenings.

One observer

Only one researcher could be on-site, which limited what could be tracked at once. The number of patients the therapist was treating at any moment, for example, couldn’t be captured reliably.

Language and culture

Sessions were logged by a teammate familiar with the local culture and translated between Arabic and English, which leaves room for meaning to shift. The two of us who did most of the initial coding weren’t familiar with the culture, and fewer than 5% of codes changed through our reconciliation sessions.

Shift coverage

Both sessions were late-evening shifts (6 or 7 to 10 pm), so how the team works at other times is unknown.

Staff access

The receptionist was unavailable beyond their working role, so we couldn’t administer the OSI to them or collect any information beyond the observation sessions.

Rapport

It varied across the staff. With limited time, it likely wasn’t fully established with the assistant, and after the OSI, the teammate who administered it raised a concern that the assistant’s answers may have leaned artificially positive.

No patient outcome data

Several of the related studies we reviewed measured clinic team success with patient outcome data. We didn’t have access to that data, and the sessions weren’t long enough to connect our measures to outcomes.

Instrument fit

We used the OSI because it was available to us. The revised version (OSI-R) would likely have fit better, but our university library didn’t have access to it, and we couldn’t hear back from its authors in time. We also kept only the items from three subscales that seemed relevant to physical therapy work, which may have skewed some scores against the modified norms.

Clinic vibe

The metric wasn’t robust enough to add insight, since both days showed similar proportions of positive and negative events.

Reflection

Despite its limitations, the approach worked better than we anticipated. Applying the metrics surfaced several areas in the team’s interactions that, if addressed, would likely reduce inefficiency.

Looking at whether the metrics would transfer, stress and communication efficiency could help assess team interactions at other clinics, or in medical settings where a doctor works with supporting staff, though the specific events, like buzzers and check-ins, would need adapting. Clinic vibe would need more factors to be useful. The metrics were also designed around an experienced team, so applying them to novices, especially communication efficiency, would take adjustment.

With more time and resources, I would want to pinpoint more specific sources of inefficiency and suggest more concrete solutions, ones that could ease the strain on staff and potentially improve outcomes for patients. That would require observing across more days and shifts, adding patient outcome data, interviewing every role, including reception, and bringing in a second on-site observer. A deeper study would also give future work a stronger basis for extending the metrics to new settings and testing whether proposed changes help.
